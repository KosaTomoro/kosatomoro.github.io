Work has been intense lately. In the middle of it, all I could feel was exhaustion and strain. With a little distance, I realized that the pressure had also forced me to rethink some things that mattered.

Pressure itself is not something to celebrate. When it stays out of control, it can damage both our health and our emotional well-being. But if we can come through a difficult period with a clearer understanding of ourselves, our industry, and the direction we want to take, then going through such a period while young may not be entirely a bad thing.

What follows are the answers I want to leave for myself after thinking about these questions repeatedly. They are not final conclusions, just a record of where I stand today.

## 1. What pressure can teach us

The most immediate effect of pressure is that it makes problems impossible to ignore.

When things are steady, it is easy to keep moving on autopilot: finish the work, collect a paycheck, wait for the next promotion, and put those vague doubts aside. Only when the pressure becomes intense do we seriously ask: Why am I still here? What do I actually want? If this job disappeared today, what would I have left?

For me, the value of this experience was not that it made me better at enduring things. It forced me to stop living on autopilot. I realized that work is only one part of life, and a job title is not an identity. What I need to build is the ability, judgment, and order in my life that do not depend on a particular organization or position.

Difficult experiences do not carry meaning automatically. They become growth only when we reflect on them, draw something useful from them, and turn that understanding into action.

## 2. How I see the industry and the times we live in

[Claude Opus 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5) was recently released. The impact of this new round of model improvements feels more immediate than any before it. AI is not just making programmers more efficient; it is changing how software is designed, produced, and consumed.

“Everyone can be a programmer” is becoming more than a slogan. More and more people can turn an idea into usable software with AI, without first studying traditional programming systematically. This does not mean programmers will disappear overnight. But much of the repetitive work in software development is likely to shrink quickly, and the way we assess the value of these jobs will change with it.

Based on what I see today, the directions I am more optimistic about are data infrastructure and physical AI infrastructure. Model capabilities will become increasingly commoditized, and the cost of producing software will keep falling. High-quality data, feedback from the real world, and infrastructure that connects models to the physical world will remain scarce.

If I accept that view, my attitude toward my current work becomes clearer: let go of my attachment to a particular position, and to traditional ways of producing software. Gradually move my skills and direction closer to AI.

Whether that immediately makes money is secondary. What matters more is first understanding what the market needs in the AI era, actively learning and using AI, and then trying to develop, debug, and solve real problems with it. That process can help me make better career and investment decisions while building capabilities that make me less dependent on employment.

On a larger scale, continued progress in AI may push society toward a new way of organizing itself. As more repetitive work is taken over, people may have more opportunities to return to themselves and reconsider how a life should be lived. That question may stay with the generation experiencing this transformation for a long time.

In the face of these changes, there are three things I want to start doing early:

1. **In mindset**: move from accepting and adapting to change toward understanding it and learning to guide it.
2. **In physical health**: exercise consistently and maintain my energy. The body is the foundation for every long-term choice.
3. **In inner life**: keep reading nourishing material and give my inner world something to draw on.

## 3. The most important question to answer under pressure

Many people believe they love the internet industry, only to discover that what attracted them was not the work itself but the high salaries, growth, and possibilities it once offered.

Recognizing that raises two questions worth asking again: What do I actually enjoy? What kind of work do I want to do?

The answer may be that I do not know the name of a particular job. There are thousands of roles in the world; I cannot try every one before choosing. What determines my direction may not be a job title at all, but a way of working I believe in—a state I sincerely want to be in.

The state I want is to be a creator: to have my own thoughts and judgment, make content or products of my own, let real people use them, and receive recognition and feedback. Then I want to keep improving what I make in response, building a positive cycle of thinking, creating, receiving feedback, and refining.

In other words, I want to spend the long term doing things that truly excite me.

At the same time, my attitude toward money deserves another look. Making a fortune, buying a luxury car, and owning things we like are default goals for many people. Yet the excitement of getting something we have wanted often fades after a week or two. Material things can improve life, but they rarely provide deep, lasting satisfaction on their own.

By comparison, there are three things I would rather pursue: clarity and order, rigorous reasoning, and simplicity.

Clothes can be ordinary, but they should be clean and tidy. Life can be modest, but it should have order. Whether speaking or acting, I want to be clear and logical. These should be more than standards for efficiency; they should become a way of working and living.

## 4. Rethinking my values

### 1. Recognizing my boundaries

We all have boundaries. Our backgrounds, upbringing, and experiences shape them, leaving inevitable limits in how we understand people and situations—as if we were living inside an invisible box.

Moving beyond those boundaries is difficult. That makes it all the more worthwhile to recognize them, challenge them, and work through them. The process matters in its own right.

Take communication as an example. Some people face clear limitations when talking to others or navigating social situations. Looking back, those limitations often relate to how they grew up. Once we recognize the problem, we can read about communication, observe people who do it well, and deliberately practice expressing ourselves more naturally and comfortably. In this way, those boundaries can gradually expand.

One important thing to do while young is to discover those boundaries early, then keep trying to step beyond them.

### 2. Staying curious and moving toward the unfamiliar

When we encounter something we do not understand, curiosity comes naturally: How does this work? How did someone else manage to do it? But often, we stop the thought with “That has nothing to do with me.”

To grow faster, I need to be bolder, more proactive, and more willing to ask questions and seek advice—to enter fields I do not yet understand.

Doing so gives me new points of reference. They may not seem useful immediately, but they could connect in the future. More importantly, staying curious and seeking advice can broaden my perspective and introduce me to excellent people. Once my perspective opens up, conversations naturally offer more chances to exchange ideas and find common ground. That is interesting in its own right.

## 5. Finding a direction in the AI era

In my view, the room for growth in traditional software is narrowing. For someone who wants a long-term future in technology, moving closer to AI is becoming almost inevitable. It requires a change in how we work, as well as in the direction we choose.

But it is difficult to identify the right entry point all at once. A more realistic path is to first enter the world of AI: learn it, use it, and then work on AI-related businesses firsthand. Understand the market by solving real problems, and gradually discover new opportunities.

Combined with curiosity, initiative, and the courage to move beyond my boundaries, a sustained period of focused work makes it more likely that an opportunity will emerge.

Of course, this takes time. My principle is simple: **be deliberate in strategy and quick in execution.**

Think the direction through instead of repeatedly changing it out of anxiety. Once the direction is clear, move quickly: take small steps, validate promptly, and keep adjusting in response to feedback.

## 6. Four things worth continuing before AI becomes pervasive

### 1. Build a record of my own life and thinking

In the future, software may become extremely cheap. More people will be able to write programs with AI, and software could even become disposable: a tool we use today and replace tomorrow.

Data is different. It is a core asset for companies, and it can become a core asset for individuals too.

An individual does not have a company's complete data infrastructure, but we can start by keeping a record of ourselves. That makes taking notes and keeping a diary important. While reading *Zeng Guofan*, I noticed a detail: when he resolved to become a sage, he began by keeping a diary consistently.

If I structure those records where practical, while protecting privacy and security, I can gradually build a personal knowledge base. In the future, AI could use that accumulated record to help me analyze problems and make decisions.

### 2. Extend my attention to firsthand sources abroad

Staying within a single information environment makes it easy to become surrounded by algorithms and secondhand accounts. Actively seeking firsthand sources from overseas can broaden my perspective and reduce the distortion that comes with repeated retelling.

For example, when following US stocks, instead of relying only on summaries, I can read company announcements, reports from professional institutions, and practitioners' public statements directly. More importantly, different information environments can help me break habitual patterns of thought and see a wider range of answers about values, career choices, and ways of living.

The point is not to trust foreign information blindly. It is to get closer to primary sources, cross-check them, and form my own judgment.

### 3. Keep reading

AI can be understood as a new kind of search engine. But more important than simply knowing how to use it are the ability to ask good questions, keep following up, and judge whether an answer is reliable.

Depth of thought, foresight, and insight still require reading and long-term reflection. With that foundation, AI can be truly useful. It can amplify the understanding we already have, but it cannot replace the work of building our own understanding.

### 4. Build a public body of work

In the future, the content we publish can become a lasting introduction to who we are. Whether starting a business or collaborating, we inevitably deal with people we do not yet know. Work accumulated over time can communicate our thinking, integrity, and abilities, making trust easier to establish.

Publishing is also a way to express ideas. It pushes me to keep learning and thinking, and may lead to real connections, collaborations, and income. The barrier to entry is lower than it seems. What matters is beginning seriously and continuing over the long term.

## A final thought

Looking back, the difficult days were not wasted. Pressure pushed me to think, and thinking helped me grow.

Everything I want to say comes down to one line:

> Think inward. Express freely.
